Welcome!

I'm a PhD student in Computer Science at the University of Michigan, where I work at the intersection of human-centered AI, HCI, affective computing, and computational mental health. My research broadly asks how computational systems represent people's experiences and behaviors, how those representations relate to people's own understanding of themselves, and how we can design intelligent systems that are useful and understandable to the people interacting with them.

About Me

I graduated from the University of Michigan in 2020 with a B.S. in Computer Science and Biopsychology, Cognition, and Neuroscience. After graduating, I worked as a software engineer at Microsoft, first on Azure and later on a research incubation team developing healthcare technology. I returned to the University of Michigan for graduate school and am now pursuing my PhD in Computer Science. My work combines computational and human-centered approaches. I'm interested in how AI systems can model complex human states, and in how people understand, interpret, and interact with the representations those systems produce, as well as with the systems themselves. I am particularly interested in the intersection of AI and mental health, and in how we can design systems that are useful, understandable, and ethical.

My Research

Human Centered Passive Monitoring

I study how people experience passive mental health monitoring in everyday life. In a recent longitudinal study, I examined how people with bipolar disorder made sense of six months of ambient-audio monitoring, including how they interpreted their data, provided context that monitoring systems could not capture, and navigated questions around privacy, control, and bystanders. This work motivates broader questions about when intelligent systems should involve users in interpreting, correcting, or supplementing computational representations of their experiences.

Speech, Affect, and Mental Health

I also study how speech and affective signals can be used to understand changes in mental health over time. My current work uses longitudinal ambient speech, self-report, and clinical assessments to investigate relationships between everyday behavior, emotion, and symptoms of bipolar disorder. I'm particularly interested in within-person variation, and understanding how models can account for individual differences in behavior and emotion.

Emotion Modeling

Another line of my work examines the relationship between computational representations of emotion and people's own reports of their emotional experiences. My recent work has studied the generalizability of emotion models trained on third-party annotations to self-reported emotion, as well as questions around variability and reliability in emotion annotation.
